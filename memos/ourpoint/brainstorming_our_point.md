## The Two Types of Exemptions

Senior property tax exemptions come in (at least) two flavors:

1. **Uncapped**: All seniors qualify regardless of income. Texas, parts of Georgia. Dollar values can be large ($2k+/year).

2. **Circuit-breaker / Income-capped**: Only low-income seniors qualify. Florida ($38k cap), many others. Dollar values tend to be smaller.

**Any paper about senior exemptions needs a theory for why both exist.** They can't both be fiscal competition for mobile retirees—the income cap rules that out for circuit-breakers.

---

## Pet Theories for Why Both Exist

### Uncapped = Fiscal Competition
Jurisdictions compete for wealthy mobile retirees who are fiscal assets (pay taxes, don't use schools). Large, uncapped exemptions attract them. Tiebout story fits.

### Circuit-breaker = Constituent Service / Fairness
Not trying to attract anyone. Goals could be:
- **Constituent service**: Keep existing low-income seniors from leaving or facing hardship. Golden handcuffs for the poor.
- **Fairness**: Seniors subsidize schools they don't use. Exemption corrects this cross-subsidy. Income cap means correction is means-tested.
- **Anti-displacement**: As property values rise, keep long-term residents in their homes.
- **Political economy**: Seniors vote. Targeted transfer to a voting bloc.

The income cap reveals that the goal isn't attracting wealthy retirees. But which of the above is the actual goal? Can we distinguish empirically?

---

## The Fairness Framing

Two ways to describe the exemption:
- "Seniors receive a subsidy" (standard framing)
- "Seniors stop subsidizing schools they don't use" (alternative)

The second framing makes the exemption sound like correcting an unfairness rather than a giveaway. But the income cap complicates it—if fairness were the goal, why not give it to all seniors?

Possible answer: Low-income seniors can least afford to subsidize others. Means-testing the fairness correction.

## Aging-In as Evidence

Florida's parcel data can show whether claimants are:
- **Aging-in**: Owned the home for years, turned 65 or income dropped
- **Moving-in**: Bought recently as an eligible senior

If mostly aging-in, the exemption isn't attracting anyone. It's a transfer to people already there.

**Limitation**: Suggestive, not causal. Without policy variation, can't rule out that stayers would have left counterfactually.

**Why it matters**: If 90% aging-in, that's strong evidence against Tiebout for circuit-breaker exemptions. The policy isn't doing what the fiscal competition story says.

## Need Another State

Florida alone can't answer the interesting questions. To compare income-capped vs. uncapped, or to get policy variation, need at least one other state.

**Candidates**:
- Georgia: Banzhaf's data (if accessible). Has within-state variation in exemption design.
- Texas: Large uncapped exemptions but limited microdata.
- State with recent policy change: Raised/lowered income cap, or switched from capped to uncapped.

**What to look for**: Parcel-level or county-level exemption data + either cross-sectional or time-series variation in design.

## Questions Still Unresolved

1. **What's the actual contribution?** Aging-in decomposition is neat but doesn't have a clear "so what."

2. **Is Florida the right setting?** Small exemption, income-capped, limited policy variation. Maybe the interesting action is in states with bigger, uncapped exemptions.

3. **What would change someone's mind?** Need to identify a belief that could be overturned by evidence.

4. **Who's the audience?** Public finance people care about incidence. Policy people care about whether exemptions "work." Political economy people care about why these policies exist.

## Threads to Pull

- **Document the typology**: How many states have circuit-breaker vs. uncapped? Is there a pattern (blue states vs. red states, high-tax vs. low-tax)?

- **Compare claiming rates**: In states with uncapped exemptions, what share of eligible seniors claim? If take-up is low even when universal, that's interesting.

- **Find a policy change**: State that switched designs or changed income threshold. Event study.

- **The political economy angle**: Why do localities adopt exemptions that (apparently) don't serve efficiency purposes? Seniors vote, but so do non-seniors. What's the political equilibrium?

## The Honest Assessment

Right now this is a project with good data (Florida) but no clear question. The data can answer some descriptive questions (who claims, aging-in vs. move-in) but nothing that feels like a contribution.

The income cap observation is the most interesting thread. It reveals that circuit-breaker exemptions aren't about fiscal competition. But turning that into a paper requires either:
- Comparing to uncapped states (need data)
- Finding policy variation within Florida (may not exist)
- Reframing as political economy (different literature, different tools)
